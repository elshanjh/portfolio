Fourteen-day challenge for existing buyers

Daily email series, 21 September to 4 October 2026

A daily email series kept about 90% of its starting audience subscribed through day 14 while selling in only one email out of three.

The goal was to move buyers of an entry-level product toward a paid programme without wearing out the relationship. The audience was about 280 customers who had already bought once.

I planned the 14-day arc, wrote every email, and built the automation. I set one rule for the series: two emails give practical value, the third makes the offer.

The clicks came where the series was designed to ask for them, on the selling emails, led by the first one at 3.2%. Unsubscribes averaged 0.7% per email and never passed 2%, low for daily sends over two weeks.

How it was built

  1. StartA tag on the contact starts the flow, with two entry conditions that filter who can come in.
  2. Days 1 to 13One email a day, with a one-day delay between each. Two emails give practical value, the third makes the offer, and the pattern repeats four times.
  3. Day 14A final email closes the challenge, and the contact leaves the flow.

286 contacts started the flow and 256 had received the final email when these numbers were taken. Nobody was removed early, so the gap is unsubscribes and a few contacts still in progress.

What I would change: the value emails had no link to click, so the next version gives each one a small next step to measure engagement between offers.

Founder story welcome series

Automated sequence of ten emails, live since 8 September 2026

A ten-email automated sequence that tells the founder’s story before any offer. The first email opened at 30.0%, and the later emails kept opening at 24% to 48% among the contacts who reached them.

The goal was to introduce subscribers to the founder and the method before any offer, so the first sale comes from trust. About 540 contacts have entered the series.

I wrote all ten emails as one continuing story, each ending on a reason to open the next.

How it was built

  1. StartThe flow starts when a contact finishes one of three earlier sequences. Each ending applies a tag, and any of the three tags lets the contact in.
  2. Email 1 and 2The second email follows one day after the first.
  3. Email 3 to 10After that, one email every two days.
  4. EndA “completed” tag is added, so later campaigns can tell who has read the whole story.

The first five emails each reached about 500 contacts, and opens moved from 30.0% on the first to 19.7% on the fifth, against an account average of about 25%. Emails six to ten have so far reached a smaller group of 65 to 80 contacts, where opens ran from 24.4% to 47.8%. The strongest was the eighth email, at 47.8% opens and 9.0% clicks.

Customer survey campaign

One campaign email with an automated thank-you

A short personal email asking customers for feedback got a 17.0% click rate, about six times the account’s campaign average of 2.8%.

The goal was to collect direct feedback from customers to guide what we offered them next. It went to about 240 customers.

I wrote the email as a personal request under the subject line “Do you have a few minutes for me?”, kept it to one ask and one link, and built an automated thank-you for everyone who responded.

27.8% opened and 17.0% clicked through to the survey. 36 people completed it, and 88.9% of them opened the thank-you email.

How it was built

  1. CampaignOne email to existing customers, linking to a survey built in Mailchimp.
  2. StartSubmitting the survey starts a short automation.
  3. Thank-youThe contact gets a thank-you email straight away.
  4. EndA “responded to survey” tag is added, so responders can be segmented in later campaigns.

Monthly newsletters

Three editions, August and September 2026

Three newsletter editions went out while the list grew by about 30%, from roughly 470 to 610 subscribers, and two of the three opened well above the account average of 25%.

I planned the content, wrote each edition and built it in Mailchimp. The first September edition underperformed, so I reworked the subject line and structure for the second, which recovered to 32.3%.

The subject lines explain most of the difference. The seasonal one opened at 18.9%. The two that named a specific problem with the reader’s own training opened at 38.5% and 32.3%.

Waitlist launch for a new product

One invitation email

A single invitation email to join the waitlist for an upcoming product reached a 3.5% click rate, above the campaign average of 2.8%.

The goal was to measure interest in a new product before launch and build a warm audience for it. The email went to about 580 subscribers.

I wrote the invitation, set up the waitlist sign-up and tagged everyone who joined so the launch could go to them first.

22.5% opened, 3.5% clicked, and only 0.3% unsubscribed.

Product name A/B test

Mailchimp A/B test, about 600 contacts

I suspected the main programme’s name was turning away a large part of its audience. A 50/50 test on purchases backed that up, and the client renamed the whole programme.

The programme’s name was built around abs, which I felt spoke mostly to men. Yet the client’s second product, a cookbook, was bought mainly by women, so many potential buyers might not see themselves in the name.

How it was built

  1. HypothesisA name built around fat loss would sell better to the whole audience than one built around abs.
  2. SplitAbout 600 contacts, divided 50/50 in Mailchimp.
  3. VersionsVersion A kept the original name. Version B used a fat-loss name, and everything that carried the name changed with it: subject line, email content and landing page. Nothing else differed.
  4. MeasurePurchase rate for each version.

The fat-loss version led to more purchases, and on that result the client changed the programme’s name across the whole business.

KYNO newsletter, built from scratch

Brevo, first issue sent 18 October 2026

KYNO, the film platform I co-founded, had a social following of about 14,000 but no email list. I set up its email programme on my own in Brevo, from the signup to the first monthly newsletter, written in Azerbaijani.

How it was built

  1. SignupA signup link shared in KYNO’s Instagram stories and posts adds people to the subscriber list.
  2. WelcomeEvery new subscriber gets a welcome email.
  3. Newsletter“Kino gündəliyi”, a monthly film journal, sent from KYNO’s own address, info@kyno.az.

Each issue follows the same structure: the month’s film news, one film suggestion from each of the five team members, a short review by one of them, and the latest podcast episode. Every film mentioned appears with its poster, and the release calendar lists only what is actually showing at Park Cinema and CinemaPlus, so readers in Azerbaijan can go and see it.

First issue

The first issue, “The first issue of Kino gündəliyi is here!”, went out on 18 October to the first 24 subscribers. All were delivered, 17 opened it, 3 clicked through and nobody unsubscribed. Brevo’s open count includes Apple Mail privacy opens, so clicks are the number I track as the list grows.

Rider reactivation flow for Bolt

Spec concept, October 2026. Written without a client and not affiliated with Bolt

A three-step flow for riders who haven’t taken a ride in 30 days. Instead of asking them to come back at random times, each message arrives at a moment when a ride actually solves a problem: rain, a match night, the evening rush.

I read almost every email I get, and when one makes me click, I go back and work out why. Usually it arrived at the right moment. That is the idea behind this flow. People who stop riding rarely need to be convinced that taxis exist. They need a reason today.

The audience is riders with at least three rides in the 60 days before they went quiet. 10% of them get no messages at all, so the flow can be judged against riders who would have come back anyway. Any rider who books a ride leaves the flow straight away.

How it is built

  1. Day 30, emailA light reminder with no discount. Many riders haven’t left, they have just drifted, and a short friendly email costs nothing.
  2. Branch for bad last tripsRiders whose last ride went wrong, such as a low rating, a driver cancellation or a long wait, get a different email instead. It asks what happened, with one-tap answers, and offers the next ride free up to €5. Each answer leads to its own follow-up.
  3. Day 33, pushOnly if there is still no ride. Sent on a trigger instead of a fixed date: bad weather, a big event in the rider’s city, or the evening rush.
  4. Day 37 onward, in-appShown the next time the rider opens the app without booking. This is the only step with a discount, 20% off one ride, because these riders are interested but something is stopping them.
  5. RulesNo more than one message from the flow every three days. Riders with an open support ticket are left out until it is answered.

The messages

Step and copyA/B test
EmailWhere are you headed this week, {first_name}?Against a live subject line, “A ride is about {eta} minutes from your door”. Judged on rides within seven days, not opens.
Email, bad last tripYour last ride wasn’t great. Can you tell us why?Against the standard reminder, for riders with a bad last trip.
Push, rainRain is on its way. Skip the wet walk home.Context triggers against a fixed send on Thursday at 17:30. Judged on rides within 24 hours.
Push, match nightEstonia play at home tonight. Get dropped right at the gate.
Push, evening rushThe bus is full. Your ride isn’t.
In-appWelcome back. Here’s 20% off your next ride.Against the same message with no discount. Shows whether the money brings riders back or goes to people who would ride anyway.

On match nights prices rise and waits get longer, which is a bad first ride back for someone who already drifted away. So the match push goes out early in the day and asks the rider to book before kick-off. Only riders who live in the city, or have ridden near the stadium before, receive it.

What I would measure

The main number is how many riders take a ride within 14 days, compared with the 10% who got nothing. After that: which step brought them back, how many are still riding 30 days later, the discount cost per returning rider, and unsubscribes and push opt-outs, so the flow doesn’t lose a channel to win back one ride.

What I would test next: riders who still ride but less often could get personal stats instead of reminders, such as “You took 14 rides last spring. Scooters are back.” People like facts about themselves more than facts about a brand.
