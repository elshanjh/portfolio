Fourteen-day challenge for existing buyers

Daily email series, 14 days

A daily email series kept about 90% of its starting audience subscribed through day 14 while selling in only one email out of three.

The goal was to move buyers of an entry-level product toward a paid programme without wearing out the relationship. The audience was about 280 customers who had already bought once.

I planned the 14-day arc, wrote every email, and built the automation. I set one rule for the series: two emails give practical value, the third makes the offer.

The clicks came where the series was designed to ask for them, on the selling emails, led by the first one at 3.2%. Unsubscribes averaged 0.7% per email and never passed 2%, low for daily sends over two weeks.

12 of the roughly 280 buyers upgraded to the paid programme, about 4.3%.

How it was built

  1. StartA tag on the contact starts the flow, with two entry conditions that filter who can come in.
  2. Days 1 to 13One email a day, with a one-day delay between each. Two emails give practical value, the third makes the offer, and the pattern repeats four times.
  3. Day 14A final email closes the challenge, and the contact leaves the flow.

286 contacts started the flow and 256 had received the final email when these numbers were taken. Nobody was removed early, so the gap is unsubscribes and a few contacts still in progress.

What I would change: the value emails had no link to click, so the next version gives each one a small next step to measure engagement between offers.

Founder story welcome series

Automated sequence of ten emails

A ten-email automated sequence that introduces new subscribers to the founder’s story. The first email opened at 30.0%, and the strongest reached 47.8%.

The goal was to introduce subscribers to the founder and the method, with no selling. About 540 contacts entered the series.

I wrote all ten emails as one continuing story, each ending on a reason to open the next.

How it was built

  1. StartThe flow starts when a contact finishes one of three earlier sequences. Each ending applies a tag, and any of the three tags lets the contact in.
  2. Email 1 and 2The second email follows one day after the first.
  3. Email 3 to 10After that, one email every two days.
  4. EndA “completed” tag is added, so later campaigns can tell who has read the whole story.

The first email opened at 30.0%, against an account average of about 25%. The strongest was the eighth email, at 47.8% opens and 9.0% clicks.

Customer survey campaign

One campaign email with an automated thank-you

A short personal email asking customers for feedback got a 17.0% click rate, about six times the account’s campaign average of 2.8%.

The goal was to collect direct feedback from customers to guide what we offered them next. It went to about 240 customers.

I wrote the email as a personal request under the subject line “Do you have a few minutes for me?”, kept it to one ask and one link, and built an automated thank-you for everyone who responded.

27.8% opened and 17.0% clicked through to the survey. 36 people completed it, and 88.9% of them opened the thank-you email.

The answers showed what customers wanted next, and the client designed and launched its next product based on them.

How it was built

  1. CampaignOne email to existing customers, linking to a survey built in Mailchimp.
  2. StartSubmitting the survey starts a short automation.
  3. Thank-youThe contact gets a thank-you email straight away.
  4. EndA “responded to survey” tag is added, so responders can be segmented in later campaigns.

Monthly newsletters

Three monthly editions

Three newsletter editions went out while the list grew by about 30%, from roughly 470 to 610 subscribers, and two of the three opened well above the account average of 25%.

I planned the content, wrote each edition and built it in Mailchimp. The second edition underperformed, so I reworked the subject line and structure for the third, which recovered to 32.3%.

The subject lines explain most of the difference. The seasonal one opened at 18.9%. The two that named a specific problem with the reader’s own training opened at 38.5% and 32.3%.

Waitlist launch for a new product

One invitation email

One invitation email built a waitlist of 20 people for an upcoming product, and 8 of them bought when it launched, a 40% conversion from waitlist to purchase.

The goal was to measure interest in a new product before launch and build a warm audience for it. The email went to about 580 subscribers.

I wrote the invitation, set up the waitlist sign-up and tagged everyone who joined so the launch could go to them first.

The invitation opened at 22.5%, its 3.5% click rate was above the account’s campaign average of 2.8%, and only 0.3% unsubscribed.

Product name A/B test

Mailchimp A/B test, about 600 contacts

I suspected the main programme’s name was turning away a large part of its audience. A 50/50 test on purchases backed that up, and the client renamed the whole programme.

The programme’s name was built around abs, which I felt spoke mostly to men. Yet the client’s second product, a cookbook, was bought mainly by women, so many potential buyers might not see themselves in the name.

How it was built

  1. HypothesisA name built around fat loss would sell better to the whole audience than one built around abs.
  2. SplitAbout 600 contacts, divided 50/50 in Mailchimp.
  3. VersionsVersion A kept the original name. Version B used a fat-loss name, and everything that carried the name changed with it: subject line, email content and landing page. Nothing else differed.
  4. MeasurePurchase rate for each version.

The fat-loss version led to more purchases, and on that result the client changed the programme’s name across the whole business.

KYNO newsletter, built from scratch

Brevo, built from scratch

KYNO, the film platform I co-founded, had a social following of about 14,000 but no email list. I set up its email programme on my own in Brevo, from the signup to the first monthly newsletter, written in Azerbaijani.

How it was built

  1. SignupA signup link shared in KYNO’s Instagram stories and posts adds people to the subscriber list.
  2. WelcomeEvery new subscriber gets a welcome email.
  3. Newsletter“Kino gündəliyi”, a monthly film journal, sent from KYNO’s own address, info@kyno.az.

The first issue brought together the month’s film news, a film suggestion from each of the five team members, a short review and the latest podcast episode, with a poster for every film mentioned. The mix changes from issue to issue, so the newsletter doesn’t feel the same every month.

First issue

The first issue, “The first issue of Kino gündəliyi is here!”, went out to the first 24 subscribers. All were delivered, 18 opened it, 3 clicked through and nobody unsubscribed. Brevo’s open count includes Apple Mail privacy opens, so clicks are the number I track as the list grows.

Rider reactivation flow for Bolt

Spec concept, October 2026. Written without a client and not affiliated with Bolt

A three-step flow for riders who haven’t taken a ride in 30 days. A reminder first, a nudge built on their own trips, and a discount only for those who still need it.

I use Bolt myself. Whenever I have tapped one of its notifications, it was because at that moment I really needed a ride. People who stopped riding already know the app. What they need is a good reason to open it.

The flow is for riders who took at least three rides before they went quiet. 10% of them get no messages, so the results can be compared with riders who would have come back anyway. Anyone who books a ride leaves the flow straight away.

How it is built

  1. Day 30, emailA light reminder with no discount. Many riders haven’t left, they have just drifted, and a short friendly email costs nothing.
  2. Day 33, pushOnly if there is still no ride. It names the place the rider went most often, so it reads like a trip they already know. It goes out outside rush hour, when there are free cars nearby and the price is normal, so the first ride back is a good one.
  3. Day 37 onward, in-appShown the next time the rider opens the app without booking. This is the only step with a discount, because these riders are interested but something is stopping them.

No rider gets more than one message every three days. The copy is written in English here. Each market’s team would adapt it to the local language and swap in their own places.

The messages

MessageWhen
EmailWhere are you headed this week, {first_name}?Day 30

What I would test

First, the in-app message with and without the discount. If riders come back just as often without it, the money is better spent elsewhere. Second, the push with the rider’s own destination against a general one, to see if the personal detail is worth it.

The main number is how many riders take a ride within 14 days, compared with the 10% who got nothing. I would also watch unsubscribes and push opt-outs, because losing someone’s email or push permission costs more than one ride.

From first purchase to second: an agentic lifecycle flow for ESTO

Spec concept, October 2026. Built from public information for my application to ESTO’s Lifecycle Marketing Manager role. Not affiliated with ESTO

More first-time ESTO customers come back for a second purchase within 90 days, with no rise in missed payments. AI drafts, launches and watches. People approve the copy and decide every next test.

Most customers meet ESTO at a partner store’s checkout. They choose Pay Later, ESTO 3 or hire purchase, and leave. Lifecycle is where a one-time buyer can become a regular.

Every ESTO product is credit, so the flow is built around one rule: repayment comes first, the next purchase second. A customer only hears about a second purchase once their first plan is on track. That protects the customer, keeps Compliance comfortable, and brings back the repeat customers ESTO actually wants.

The flow

What AI owns, what a person reviews

AI takes the volume and the speed: data pulls, drafts, variants and daily monitoring. People keep anything that touches credit risk, legal claims, or the first send to real customers.

1. Build cohort
AIPulls first-purchase events and groups customers by product, store category and market.
PersonThe lifecycle manager approves the segment definitions once. Data spot-checks the query.
2. Repayment check
AINothing. Eligibility is a fixed set of rules, not a model.
PersonCompliance signs off the rules, and any change goes back to them.
3. Draft
AICopy variants per segment and channel, in each market’s languages, with the required credit wording.
PersonThe lifecycle manager edits for voice, a native speaker checks each language, and Compliance approves any claim about fees or terms.
4. Launch
AISchedules the approved variants and sets up the holdout group.
PersonApproves the first send of every new variant. After that, AI only rotates approved variants.
5. Monitor
AIWatches delivery, clicks, unsubscribes, complaints and payment status every day, and pauses any send that crosses an agreed limit.
PersonGets the alert and decides whether to resume.
6. Learn
AIReads the results, writes a one-page summary and proposes the next test.
PersonDecides what to test next, or stops the flow.

The repayment check is not AI on purpose. Deciding who is ready to be offered more credit should be predictable, explainable and easy to audit. A set of rules is all three. A model writing copy is none of them.

The first test

The first test answers one question: does the flow cause extra second purchases, or would those customers have come back anyway?

  • A share of eligible first-time customers, 10% to start, gets only the messages about their payments. Everyone else gets the flow.
  • The main number is a second purchase within 90 days. The lift is the gap between the flow group and the holdout, not the raw conversion rate.
  • Late or missed payments, unsubscribes and complaints are guardrails. If any of them gets worse in the flow group, the flow is not a win, whatever the lift.
  • Before launch, the team agrees the smallest lift worth acting on and works out the sample size from today’s baseline. That sets how long the test runs.
  • If the lift is significant and no guardrail gets worse, the flow stays and scales. If not, one thing changes and the test runs again.

Once the flow proves it adds purchases, later tests compare versions of it. For example, a nudge after the first instalment is paid against a nudge only after the whole plan is repaid.

Reporting and monitoring

One dashboard follows each weekly group of new customers: first purchases by product and market, the share that passes the repayment check, second purchases at 30, 60 and 90 days against the holdout, the guardrails, and the cost of each extra second purchase by channel, so the budget follows what works.

On top of that, AI posts a short daily summary to the team channel, alerts a person when a number crosses its limit, and drafts the weekly results write-up for the lifecycle manager to edit before it goes to stakeholders. The data could run through the stack in the job post: RudderStack into Mixpanel or Databricks, with sends in Customer.io.

What I would check with the team

  • Which product most first-time customers choose, and whether repeat behaviour differs by product
  • How many customers have given marketing consent, per channel and market
  • Which channels are live today, and what is already set up in the messaging platform
  • The rules Compliance applies to credit marketing in each Baltic market
  • Whether repeat purchases are the right first goal, or whether onboarding, repayment or reactivation matters more right now
