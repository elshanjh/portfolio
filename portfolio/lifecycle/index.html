I work in Mailchimp, Brevo and HubSpot, design in Canva and Figma, edit HTML, and use AI tools for drafting, localisation and campaign analysis. I hold HubSpot certificates in email marketing and a certificate in SQL for data science from UC Davis.

The full programme, from signup to paid programme

Lifecycle programme, Mailchimp, three months

For a health and fitness client, I wrote and built 35+ emails across 10+ automated Mailchimp journeys, sent in Estonian. Each piece on this page covers one part. This one shows how they worked together, from a first signup to the paid programme.

The client sold an entry-level product, a cookbook and a paid programme. The goal was a clear path from first signup to the paid programme that didn’t wear out the list, and a way to hear from customers so their answers could shape what came next.

The map

Before building, I mapped the whole programme: where contacts come from, which tag each step applies, and where every path leads.

How it works

  1. Three ways inContacts arrive through a signup form, a free download or a cookbook purchase. Each way in applies its own tag, so every later email knows where the contact came from.
  2. Follow-up by sourceForm leads get follow-ups toward the paid programme. Download leads get follow-ups that invite them to fill in the form, and those who do join the form path. Cookbook buyers get a recipe series.
  3. Founder storyWhen any of these ends without a programme purchase, the contact enters the ten-email founder story series.
  4. UpsellCookbook buyers also get the 14-day challenge, which leads to the programme offer.
  5. PurchaseA programme purchase at any point tags the contact as a programme customer and starts onboarding.
  6. AlongsideThe monthly newsletter, the customer survey, the waitlist, the name A/B test and a time-limited cookbook offer to leads ran as campaigns around these journeys.

Segments

Tags record what a contact did. Segments sort the whole list by where each person stands, so every send has a clear audience. Leads were sorted by engagement, customers by what they bought.

SegmentRuleWhat they got
LeadsHigh intentNo purchase, two or more clicks in the last 30 daysThe programme offer first, and launches before anyone else
LeadsEngagedNo purchase, opened or clicked in the last 60 daysFollow-ups, the newsletter and launch emails
LeadsNewJoined in the last 30 daysThe entry sequences and the founder story, before any offer
LeadsInactiveNo opens or clicks in 90 daysThe newsletter only, kept apart from offers
CustomersCookbookBought the cookbookThe recipe series and the 14-day challenge
CustomersProgrammeBought the paid programmeOnboarding, and no more programme offers
CustomersMore than one productBought two or more productsLeft out of offers for products they already own
CancelledCancelled or stopped a purchaseLeft out of all offers

Three rules keep the segments clean. A buyer leaves the lead segments as soon as they buy, so customers never get a lead offer. A lead sits in one engagement level at a time, and the highest one wins. And cancelled contacts are left out of every offer, so nobody is sold to straight after leaving.

Decisions, including what I didn’t send

Most of the work was deciding who should not get an email, and when. These are the calls that shaped the programme.

  • No offer in the welcome series. New subscribers hadn’t met the founder yet. Ten emails built trust in the person and the method first, and the offer came later in the journey.
  • Two value emails for every offer. Challenge buyers had already paid once, and a daily offer would wear out that relationship. Unsubscribes stayed at 0.7% per email across 14 daily sends.
  • A faster start, then more space. The welcome series sent its first two emails a day apart, then moved to every two days, so the story kept its pull without crowding the inbox.
  • Inactive leads were left alone. People who had stopped opening got the newsletter only. Sending them offers would risk complaints and hurt delivery for everyone else.
  • Nothing sold to cancelled customers. Selling to someone straight after they leave tells them nobody listened.
  • The waitlist heard first. The launch went to the 20 people who raised their hand before anyone else, and 8 of them bought.
  • One ask in the survey. One ask and one link, under a personal subject line, so the request was easy to say yes to. It drew a 17.0% click rate.
  • A newsletter rule, changed after a miss. The seasonal subject line opened at 18.9%. The next edition went back to naming one problem with the reader’s own training, and recovered to 32.3%.

Where the revenue comes from

The map also set out four sales opportunities, so each journey had a commercial job:

  • Upsell: cookbook buyers to the paid programme
  • Cross-sell: programme customers to the cookbook
  • Down-sell: leads who don’t buy the programme to a lower-priced offer, such as the cookbook
  • Re-engagement: inactive leads and customers back through the newsletter

Looking back

The programme covered every way in, through to onboarding. Looking back, I would add two journeys next time:

  • A checkout reminder for people who start a purchase and don’t finish it
  • A dedicated win-back flow, so inactive contacts get more than the newsletter before they become dead weight on the list

I would also give every value email in the challenge a small link to click, so engagement between offers can be measured, and track revenue per journey rather than opens and clicks alone.

Fourteen-day challenge for existing buyers

Daily email series, 14 days

A daily email series kept about 90% of its starting audience subscribed through day 14 while selling in only one email out of three.

The goal was to move buyers of an entry-level product toward a paid programme without wearing out the relationship. The audience was about 280 customers who had already bought once.

I planned the 14-day arc, wrote every email, and built the automation. I set one rule for the series: two emails give practical value, the third makes the offer.

The clicks came where the series was designed to ask for them, on the selling emails, led by the first one at 3.2%. Unsubscribes averaged 0.7% per email and never passed 2%, low for daily sends over two weeks.

12 of the roughly 280 buyers upgraded to the paid programme, about 4.3%.

How it was built

  1. StartA tag on the contact starts the flow, with two entry conditions that filter who can come in.
  2. Days 1 to 13One email a day, with a one-day delay between each. Two emails give practical value, the third makes the offer, and the pattern repeats four times.
  3. Day 14A final email closes the challenge, and the contact leaves the flow.

286 contacts started the flow and 256 had received the final email when these numbers were taken. Nobody was removed early, so the gap is unsubscribes and a few contacts still in progress.

What I would change: the value emails had no link to click, so the next version gives each one a small next step to measure engagement between offers.

Founder story welcome series

Automated sequence of ten emails

A ten-email automated sequence that introduces new subscribers to the founder’s story. The first email opened at 30.0%, and the strongest reached 47.8%.

The goal was to introduce subscribers to the founder and the method, with no selling. About 540 contacts entered the series.

I wrote all ten emails as one continuing story, each ending on a reason to open the next.

How it was built

  1. StartThe flow starts when a contact finishes one of three earlier sequences. Each ending applies a tag, and any of the three tags lets the contact in.
  2. Email 1 and 2The second email follows one day after the first.
  3. Email 3 to 10After that, one email every two days.
  4. EndA “completed” tag is added, so later campaigns can tell who has read the whole story.

The first email opened at 30.0%, against an account average of about 25%. The strongest was the eighth email, at 47.8% opens and 9.0% clicks.

Customer survey campaign

One campaign email with an automated thank-you

A short personal email asking customers for feedback got a 17.0% click rate, about six times the account’s campaign average of 2.8%.

The goal was to collect direct feedback from customers to guide what we offered them next. It went to about 240 customers.

I wrote the email as a personal request under the subject line “Do you have a few minutes for me?”, kept it to one ask and one link, and built an automated thank-you for everyone who responded.

27.8% opened and 17.0% clicked through to the survey. 36 people completed it, and 88.9% of them opened the thank-you email.

The answers showed what customers wanted next, and the client designed and launched its next product based on them.

How it was built

  1. CampaignOne email to existing customers, linking to a survey built in Mailchimp.
  2. StartSubmitting the survey starts a short automation.
  3. Thank-youThe contact gets a thank-you email straight away.
  4. EndA “responded to survey” tag is added, so responders can be segmented in later campaigns.

Product name A/B test

Mailchimp A/B test, about 600 contacts

I suspected the main programme’s name was turning away a large part of its audience. A 50/50 test on purchases backed that up, and the client renamed the whole programme.

The programme’s name was built around abs, which I felt spoke mostly to men. Yet the client’s second product, a cookbook, was bought mainly by women, so many potential buyers might not see themselves in the name.

How it was built

  1. HypothesisA name built around fat loss would sell better to the whole audience than one built around abs.
  2. SplitAbout 600 contacts, divided 50/50 in Mailchimp.
  3. VersionsVersion A kept the original name. Version B used a fat-loss name, and everything that carried the name changed with it: subject line, email content and landing page. Nothing else differed.
  4. MeasurePurchase rate for each version.

The fat-loss version led to more purchases, and on that result the client changed the programme’s name across the whole business.

How it was built

Win-back flow for dormant card users, built in Klaviyo

Practice build, November 2026. Lumo is a fictional brand. Built and tested in Klaviyo with test data, never sent to real people

A three-email flow for users of a money app who stopped using their debit card. I wrote the audience logic in SQL, rebuilt it in Klaviyo, and tested every rule against 40 test users made to break it.

Lumo is an Estonian money app with a debit card. Active users pay with the card at least once a week. The flow is for dormant users: people who made 3 or more card payments but none in the last 60 days. The goal is to get 8% of them to make one card payment within 30 days.

Some people must never get these emails: anyone without marketing consent, with a closed, frozen or under-review account, with a blocked card, or with an open complaint. People who signed up but never paid are left out on purpose. They need a different flow, one about getting started.

Who gets it: the SQL

I built a small test database with two tables, users and transactions, and wrote the query that finds the audience.

SELECT u.user_id, u.email, u.first_name,
       COUNT(t.transaction_id) AS total_transactions,
       MAX(t.transaction_date) AS last_transaction
FROM users AS u
JOIN transactions AS t ON t.user_id = u.user_id
WHERE u.account_status = 'open'
  AND u.card_status = 'active'
  AND u.marketing_consent = 'yes'
  AND u.complained = 'no'
  AND u.email LIKE '%_@_%._%'
GROUP BY u.user_id
HAVING COUNT(t.transaction_id) >= 3
   AND MAX(t.transaction_date) <= DATE('2026-11-04', '-60 days')
ORDER BY last_transaction;

The first version returned 17 users instead of 16. One of them had an email address with no @ sign, so I added the last rule in the WHERE part.

How it is built

In Klaviyo, a segment uses the same rules as the SQL and updates itself every day. It matched the SQL at 16 users. Joining the segment starts the flow.

  1. Day 0, reminderNo offer. A short note on what the card still does, because many people haven’t left, they have just drifted.
  2. Day 4, one featureRound-ups, which save the change from every card payment. One concrete reason to pay with the card again.
  3. Day 9, small offer€5 back on a payment of €10 or more within 7 days, and a link to tell us why Lumo isn’t right anymore.
EmailWhen
ReminderYour Lumo card is still in your walletDay 0
FeatureYou can save €10 a month without doing anythingDay 4
OfferGet €5 back on your next card payment!Day 9
  • The cheapest message goes first. Only people who ignored the first two emails get the €5, so Lumo doesn’t pay people who would have come back anyway.
  • People leave as soon as they pay. Before every email, flow filters check the profile again. A payment updates the last transaction date, and the next email doesn’t go out. The same filters stop the flow if the account is frozen, the card is blocked or consent is withdrawn.
  • Once every 180 days. Someone can be won back again later, but can’t collect the €5 every few months.
  • Small things. No one gets two Lumo emails within 16 hours, every link has UTM tags so returning users can be traced to the flow, and people without a first name get “Hi there”.

What testing caught

The test users included traps on purpose: a duplicate person, a broken email, a user with no name, users right on the 60-day line, and users who fail each exclusion rule one at a time.

What happenedWhat I did
Duplicate person. Klaviyo skipped both rows of a duplicate instead of merging them, so a user who should get the flow silently disappeared.Removed the duplicate before import. In a real setup this belongs in the SQL, by matching emails in lowercase with spaces removed.
Consent. Klaviyo’s “can receive email marketing” rule includes people who never subscribed. 3 users without marketing consent would have got the flow.Filtered on the app’s own consent field. For a fintech, marketing without a clear opt-in is a GDPR risk.
Segment rules. Two rules were joined with OR instead of AND, which put 27 people in the segment instead of 16.Changed to AND and checked the count against the SQL again.
Missing data. The first import didn’t bring in the custom fields, so the segment had nothing to filter on.Imported again with every field and the right types for numbers and dates, then checked one profile.
Delay. After a test payment, the user stayed in the segment for about 15 minutes.No risk to the flow, because the filters check the profile itself just before each email.
Sender name. The emails went out under the account’s default sender, not Lumo.Set the sender to Lumo on all three. In fintech, an unexpected sender looks like phishing.

I also checked the greeting for a user with and without a first name, the exit rule with a test payment, and the layout in Gmail on a phone in dark mode.

What I would add next

First, a holdout group: 10% of dormant users who get no emails, using Klaviyo’s random sample filter. The main number would be how many users make a card payment within 30 days, compared with that group. Second, payments sent from the app as live events instead of an imported field, so people leave the flow within minutes of paying. For a buy now, pay later product, I would also add a rule that never emails anyone with an overdue payment.

No results are shown because nothing was sent. What this piece shows is that the audience and the rules are right.

Rider reactivation flow for Bolt

Spec concept, October 2026. Written without a client and not affiliated with Bolt

A three-step flow for riders who haven’t taken a ride in 30 days. A reminder first, a nudge built on their own trips, and a discount only for those who still need it.

I use Bolt myself. Whenever I have tapped one of its notifications, it was because at that moment I really needed a ride. People who stopped riding already know the app. What they need is a good reason to open it.

The flow is for riders who took at least three rides before they went quiet. 10% of them get no messages, so the results can be compared with riders who would have come back anyway. Anyone who books a ride leaves the flow straight away.

How it is built

  1. Day 30, emailA light reminder with no discount. Many riders haven’t left, they have just drifted, and a short friendly email costs nothing.
  2. Day 33, pushOnly if there is still no ride. It names the place the rider went most often, so it reads like a trip they already know. It goes out outside rush hour, when there are free cars nearby and the price is normal, so the first ride back is a good one.
  3. Day 37 onward, in-appShown the next time the rider opens the app without booking. This is the only step with a discount, because these riders are interested but something is stopping them.

No rider gets more than one message every three days. The copy is written in English here. Each market’s team would adapt it to the local language and swap in their own places.

The messages

MessageWhen
EmailWhere are you headed this week, {first_name}?Day 30
PushHeading to {top_destination}? Your ride is a few taps away.Day 33, outside rush hour
In-appWelcome back. Here’s 20% off your next ride, up to €4.Next app open from day 37

What I would test

First, the in-app message with and without the discount. If riders come back just as often without it, the money is better spent elsewhere. Second, the push with the rider’s own destination against a general one, to see if the personal detail is worth it.

The main number is how many riders take a ride within 14 days, compared with the 10% who got nothing. I would also watch unsubscribes and push opt-outs, because losing someone’s email or push permission costs more than one ride.

From first purchase to second: an agentic lifecycle flow for ESTO

Spec concept, October 2026. Built from public information for my application to ESTO’s Lifecycle Marketing Manager role. Not affiliated with ESTO

More first-time ESTO customers come back for a second purchase within 90 days, with no rise in missed payments. AI drafts, launches and watches. People approve the copy and decide every next test.

Most customers meet ESTO at a partner store’s checkout. They choose Pay Later, ESTO 3 or hire purchase, and leave. Lifecycle is where a one-time buyer can become a regular.

Every ESTO product is credit, so the flow is built around one rule: repayment comes first, the next purchase second. A customer only hears about a second purchase once their first plan is on track. That protects the customer, keeps Compliance comfortable, and brings back the repeat customers ESTO actually wants.

The flow

What AI owns, what a person reviews

AI takes the volume and the speed: data pulls, drafts, variants and daily monitoring. People keep anything that touches credit risk, legal claims, or the first send to real customers.

1. Build cohort
AIPulls first-purchase events and groups customers by product, store category and market.
PersonThe lifecycle manager approves the segment definitions once. Data spot-checks the query.
2. Repayment check
AINothing. Eligibility is a fixed set of rules, not a model.
PersonCompliance signs off the rules, and any change goes back to them.
3. Draft
AICopy variants per segment and channel, in each market’s languages, with the required credit wording.
PersonThe lifecycle manager edits for voice, a native speaker checks each language, and Compliance approves any claim about fees or terms.
4. Launch
AISchedules the approved variants and sets up the holdout group.
PersonApproves the first send of every new variant. After that, AI only rotates approved variants.
5. Monitor
AIWatches delivery, clicks, unsubscribes, complaints and payment status every day, and pauses any send that crosses an agreed limit.
PersonGets the alert and decides whether to resume.
6. Learn
AIReads the results, writes a one-page summary and proposes the next test.
PersonDecides what to test next, or stops the flow.

The repayment check is not AI on purpose. Deciding who is ready to be offered more credit should be predictable, explainable and easy to audit. A set of rules is all three. A model writing copy is none of them.

The first test

The first test answers one question: does the flow cause extra second purchases, or would those customers have come back anyway?

  • A share of eligible first-time customers, 10% to start, gets only the messages about their payments. Everyone else gets the flow.
  • The main number is a second purchase within 90 days. The lift is the gap between the flow group and the holdout, not the raw conversion rate.
  • Late or missed payments, unsubscribes and complaints are guardrails. If any of them gets worse in the flow group, the flow is not a win, whatever the lift.
  • Before launch, the team agrees the smallest lift worth acting on and works out the sample size from today’s baseline. That sets how long the test runs.
  • If the lift is significant and no guardrail gets worse, the flow stays and scales. If not, one thing changes and the test runs again.

Once the flow proves it adds purchases, later tests compare versions of it. For example, a nudge after the first instalment is paid against a nudge only after the whole plan is repaid.

Reporting and monitoring

One dashboard follows each weekly group of new customers: first purchases by product and market, the share that passes the repayment check, second purchases at 30, 60 and 90 days against the holdout, the guardrails, and the cost of each extra second purchase by channel, so the budget follows what works.

On top of that, AI posts a short daily summary to the team channel, alerts a person when a number crosses its limit, and drafts the weekly results write-up for the lifecycle manager to edit before it goes to stakeholders. The data could run through the stack in the job post: RudderStack into Mixpanel or Databricks, with sends in Customer.io.

What I would check with the team

  • Which product most first-time customers choose, and whether repeat behaviour differs by product
  • How many customers have given marketing consent, per channel and market
  • Which channels are live today, and what is already set up in the messaging platform
  • The rules Compliance applies to credit marketing in each Baltic market
  • Whether repeat purchases are the right first goal, or whether onboarding, repayment or reactivation matters more right now
